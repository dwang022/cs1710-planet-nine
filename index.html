00 — Introduction

Nine planets. Then eight. Now, perhaps, nine again

The point of this section

For 76 years the solar system had nine planets. In 2006 the International Astronomical Union adopted a definition of the word, and Pluto no longer qualified. Ten years later, two astronomers argued that there is a ninth planet after all: not Pluto, but a world several times more massive than Earth, far beyond Neptune, that no one has yet seen.

1 / 5

The nine planets of the twentieth century

Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and Pluto.

Pluto, discovered in 1930, was by far the smallest. For sixty years it was the only known object in its part of the solar system, so its status was never questioned.

2 / 5

Pluto's neighbourhood fills up

From 1992 onward, surveys found many icy bodies in the same region as Pluto, now called the Kuiper belt. In 2005 one of them, Eris, turned out to be about Pluto's size, and was later shown to be more massive.

Either the list of planets would keep growing, or the word needed a definition.

3 / 5

2006: the IAU defines "planet"

A planet orbits the Sun, is massive enough to be round under its own gravity, and has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit.

Pluto meets the first two conditions. It fails the third: it shares its region with thousands of other bodies and is not the most massive of them.

4 / 5

Eight planets, and a new category

Pluto was reclassified as a dwarf planet, alongside Ceres and Eris. Nothing about Pluto changed; the definition did.

5 / 5

2016: a ninth planet is proposed

Two astronomers at Caltech noticed that several of the most distant known objects have orbits that appear to be aligned, as if a large unseen body were shaping them. They proposed Planet Nine: five to ten times Earth's mass, hundreds of times farther from the Sun than Neptune, and so far undetected.

If it exists, it would satisfy all three conditions. Whether it exists is the question the rest of this page examines.

What counts as a planet

The International Astronomical Union's 2006 definition (Resolution B5, adopted 24 August 2006). A planet is a celestial body that:

  • 1
    Is in orbit around the Sun. Moons orbit a planet instead, so they are excluded at the first condition.
  • 2
    Is round under its own gravity. It has enough mass for its self-gravity to pull it into a nearly round shape, which the resolution calls hydrostatic equilibrium.
  • 3
    Has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit. It is the dominant body in its region, having swept up, ejected or captured most of what shared the orbit.A dwarf planet meets conditions 1 and 2 but not 3, and is not a moon. Everything else orbiting the Sun is a small body.

How far away is all this?

Scroll, and the picture zooms out. The Sun stays at the left edge; everything else sits at its true distance from it. The drawn sizes are not to scale.

1 / 7

Start at Earth

Earth orbits 1 AU from the Sun: about 150 million km, or eight minutes at the speed of light. That distance is the unit used for everything on this page.

Mercury, Venus and Mars all lie within two of them.

2 / 7

The giant planets

Jupiter orbits at about 5 AU and Saturn near 10. The four rocky planets are already crowding toward the Sun's glow.

3 / 7

The edge of the familiar

Uranus at 19 AU, Neptune at 30 AU. Sunlight takes four hours to reach Neptune. All eight planets fit inside this circle.

4 / 7

Pluto and the Kuiper belt

Pluto averages 39 AU, but its orbit is a stretched oval: it passes inside Neptune's orbit at one end and reaches 49 AU at the other. The region it shares with thousands of smaller icy bodies is the Kuiper belt.

5 / 7

The extreme objects

Each thin line is one of the well-measured distant objects this page is about, drawn from its closest approach to the Sun to its farthest point. The rock marks its average distance. All of them pass near the Kuiper belt, then travel outward for hundreds or thousands of AU.

Point at one to see its numbers.

6 / 7

Where Planet Nine would be

The prediction is a range, not a point. On its best-fit orbit the planet would travel between about 320 and 600 AU from the Sun. Allowing for the uncertainty in that orbit, it could be anywhere from roughly 200 to 900 AU out. At those distances sunlight takes days to arrive, and very little of it is reflected back.

7 / 7

The farthest of them

The most distant of these objects reaches — from the Sun at the far end of its orbit. At this scale the eight planets sit inside the Sun's glow at the left edge.

AU — the ruler

One AU is Earth to the Sun, about 93 million miles. Neptune is 30 AU out. These objects reach hundreds. Voyager 1, after 48 years, is at about 170.

Orbits are ovals, not circles

Every object has a near end and a far end. It moves fast through the near end and crawls through the far one, so it spends most of its life far away.

"Which way it leans"

Because the oval is long, it points somewhere. That direction — where the near end sits — is the single number this whole page is about.

02 — The claim

They lean the same way

The point of this section

The far ends of the solar system are full of small icy objects on long, oval orbits. Their orbits nearly all swing closest to the Sun on the same side. If nothing were steering them, they would be spread evenly all the way around.

04 — The search

So why hasn't anyone found it?

The point of this section

Telescopes have photographed almost the entire sky, but only about —% of it deeply enough to see something as faint as Planet Nine. —% of its predicted path lies outside that small, deep part.

This oval is the whole sky

The faint curve is the ecliptic, the path the Sun and planets follow across the sky.

2 / 9

Planet Nine would be somewhere on the green line

This is its predicted orbit, traced across the sky (Brown & Batygin, 2021). The thin lines show how uncertain the prediction is.

Nobody knows where on the line it is right now.

3 / 9

Telescopes have photographed nearly all of it

Every shaded area has been covered by at least one large sky survey, and together they cover practically the whole sky.

So why hasn't anyone spotted it?

4 / 9

Because a photo can be too shallow

Planet Nine would be extremely faint: magnitude 22 to 25. On this scale bigger means fainter, and your eye gives out at about 6.

Each area is now shaded by how faint its photos can see. Brighter means deeper. Only a few small patches are bright. Most of the sky has only been photographed to about magnitude 22.

5 / 9

Only these slivers are deep enough

Just two surveys reach magnitude 23.5, the depth needed to catch a faint Planet Nine. Together they cover about —% of the sky.

6 / 9

Most of the path has never been searched deeply

The orange stretches are where the predicted path crosses sky that has only been photographed shallowly.

That is —% of the path. A faint planet there could easily have slipped through.

7 / 9

And part of it is lost in the Milky Way

The pale band is our own galaxy, seen edge-on. Where the path crosses it, there are so many stars that picking out one faint, slow-moving dot is nearly hopeless, even in a deep photo.

8 / 9

The search that could settle it

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory (pink outline) is surveying the southern sky to magnitude 24.5 from 2025 to 2035. That is deep enough.

It covers —% of the unsearched stretches of the path. It should either find Planet Nine or leave very few places for it to hide.
