The evidence for Planet Nine

Daniel WangGabriel GonzalezRussell Rui Luo

Far beyond Neptune, astronomers have found distant icy objects whose unusual orbits raise an intriguing possibility: could another planet be hiding in our solar system?

A planet no one has seen

Known as Planet Nine, this hypothetical world has never been directly observed. It was proposed to explain patterns in how these distant objects move.

A question still open

Scientists disagree about whether those patterns point to an undiscovered planet, to bias in where telescopes happen to look, or to something else entirely.

01 — The sample

A handful of worlds, impossibly far away

Beyond Neptune lies a swarm of small, icy bodies called trans-Neptunian objects (TNOs). A few dozen of them travel on extreme orbits that carry them hundreds of times farther from the Sun than Earth. Over billions of years, the giant planets' gravity should have spread the directions of these orbits evenly around the Sun. Some astronomers think the orbits are bunched together instead, and that an unseen planet could be the cause. Others suspect the pattern comes from where telescopes happened to look. This section introduces the objects that every later chart is built from.

The main takeaway

The evidence rests on a small group of faint, very distant objects. With the default filters their orbits look more lined up than chance usually produces, but that can shift as you change the sample, and it depends on where telescopes have looked.

04 — The search

Where we have actually looked

Flattened map of the entire sky. Shaded patches are where big surveys have hunted. The green curve is where Planet Nine would have to be.

The pale band is the Milky Way. Star fields there are so crowded that a slow-moving object is very hard to pick out — and the green path crosses it twice. Footprints are simplified to declination bands: good enough to see who looked where, not for precise coverage.
