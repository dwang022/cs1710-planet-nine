AU — the ruler

One AU is Earth to the Sun, about 93 million miles. Neptune is 30 AU out. These objects reach hundreds. Voyager 1, after 48 years, is at about 170.

Orbits are ovals, not circles

Every object has a near end and a far end. It moves fast through the near end and crawls through the far one, so it spends most of its life far away.

"Which way it leans"

Because the oval is long, it points somewhere. That direction — where the near end sits — is the single number this whole page is about.

02 — The claim

They lean the same way

Each loop is one object's path around the Sun. The dot is where it comes closest. If nothing were herding them, those dots would ring the Sun evenly.

How do I read these charts?

The left chart is the solar system seen from above, with the Sun at the centre. Each bright arc is the part of one object's orbit nearest the Sun, and the dot on it is the closest point — the direction that orbit leans. The faint lines continue outward, mostly off the edge, because these orbits are enormous.

The right chart throws away everything except that one direction. Each object becomes a dot placed by its angle, 0° to 360°. Distance from the centre means nothing here; it only stops dots from overlapping.

What to look for: if nothing were organising these objects, the dots would be spread evenly around the circle. Instead they bunch on one side. The white arrow is the average direction, and the longer it is, the more the objects agree.

Everything is connected. Point at any object in any chart on this page and it lights up in all the others at the same time.

03 — The test

Could this just be luck?

We built 4,000 fake solar systems with the same number of objects pointing in totally random directions. Grey is what luck looks like. Blue is reality.

How do I read this chart?

We take the same number of objects you have selected, point them in completely random directions, and measure how bunched up they look. Then we do that four thousand more times.

Each grey bar counts how many of those random universes came out at a given level of agreement. The pile shows what coincidence normally produces. The blue line is the real objects.

If the blue line sits inside the grey pile, the real pattern is unremarkable. The further right it sits, the worse chance works as an explanation.

04 — The search

So why hasn't anyone found it?

Almost the whole sky has been photographed. That turns out not to be the problem. The problem is how deep those photographs go.

Wide or deep — no survey is both

Each circle is one survey. Further right means it photographed more of the sky; further up means it could see fainter things. The green band is how faint Planet Nine is expected to be.
Only optical surveys appear here. Infrared searches like WISE measure brightness in a different way, so their numbers cannot be compared on this scale. Magnitudes run backwards by convention: a bigger number means a fainter object.

And the one gap that depth cannot fix

Even a deep survey struggles in one place: the crowded band of the Milky Way. This map shows the whole sky flattened onto a page, with Planet Nine's possible path running across it.

How do I read this map?

This is the entire night sky, flattened onto a page the way a world map flattens the globe. Left to right is the direction you would turn to face; up and down is how high above the horizon.

Shaded patches are the regions each telescope survey has actually photographed. The green line is the path Planet Nine would follow if it exists. Blue dots are the directions the real objects lean — the same objects you selected above.

The pale diagonal band is the Milky Way. It is so dense with stars that spotting one slow-moving dot is nearly hopeless, and the green path crosses it twice. That is where the search still has gaps.

The pale band is the Milky Way. Star fields there are so crowded that a slow-moving object is very hard to pick out — and the green path crosses it twice.
