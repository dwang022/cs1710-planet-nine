The orbits lean the same way

The far ends of the solar system are full of small icy objects on long, oval orbits, and nearly all of them swing closest to the Sun on the same side. If nothing were steering them, they would be spread evenly all the way around.

Boil it down to one arrow

Average all the directions together. Dots spread evenly around the rim cancel out and leave a stub. The more they agree, the longer the white arrow gets. If every object pointed the same way, it would reach the rim.

The proposed explanation

Astronomers proposed an unseen planet about 7 times Earth's mass, far beyond Neptune. Its own orbit, drawn in this colour, points roughly the opposite way.

Over billions of years its gravity would herd the small orbits onto the far side from itself. That is where the white arrow points. The exhibit below shows how.

Herd the orbits

Thirty-six orbits start out pointing at random. Place a planet, choose how heavy it is, and run four billion years. The planet turns every orbit to swing about the direction opposite its own, and any orbit that points the planet's way crosses its path and is eventually thrown out.

A cartoon of the mechanism in Batygin & Brown (2016), not a simulation of gravity. In the real dynamics the surviving orbits are protected by resonances with the planet and swing about anti-alignment over hundreds of millions of years. The planet sits at 460 AU with the published eccentricity; only its direction and mass are yours. Each setting is seeded, so re-running it gives the same result.

So far

A planet pointing one way would leave a crowd of orbits pointing the other. The real orbits do point one way. The question is whether that could have happened by chance, and whether the telescopes that found them could have manufactured the pattern. That is the next page.
