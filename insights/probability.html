Where the telescopes looked

The test above assumes an object pointing any direction was equally likely to be found. It was not: a distant object is only bright enough to see near its closest point, so a telescope finds the objects whose near ends lie where it happened to look. Here is what that does to a population with no pattern at all.

This is the streetlight effect: you find your keys where the light is. Real surveys are far more complicated than a few clean wedges, and the papers below disagree precisely about how much of the real pattern this explains. Shankman et al. (2017) showed that the eight distant objects from one survey that recorded exactly where it pointed are consistent with no clustering at all; Brown & Batygin (2019) modelled the biases of the whole sample and found the clustering survives.

What the papers conclude

Each row is one study's answer to the same question: how often would chance alone produce the alignment it measured? The studies differ in which objects they count, and in how they correct for where the telescopes looked, which is why they land in such different places.

Odds are read from each paper's abstract: Batygin & Brown (2016) give a 0.007% chance for their six objects; Brown & Batygin (2019) 0.2% after modelling survey bias; Brown & Batygin (2021) a 99.6% significance; Napier et al. (2021) report that a uniform population is consistent with their 14 bias-tracked objects at probabilities between 17% and 94% depending on the cut. This site's own two rows use the plain test built above, with no bias correction, so they are an upper bound on how surprising the pattern is.

So far

The lining up is real in the well-measured orbits and gone in the full set, and some of it may be an artefact of where telescopes pointed. If a planet is there, the cleanest way to settle the argument is to find it. The last part of this site is about that search: how it is done, what else could explain the pattern, how the argument has gone, and what comes next.
