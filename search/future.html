When could we know?

Dates after 2026 are our estimates.

  1. June 2026
    Rubin's survey begins. By late October it had turned up a probable new dwarf planet, 2026 ED138, the first of what should be tens of thousands of new distant objects.
  2. 2027
    Most of the southern path photographed deeply at least once. One image is not a detection. The planet has to be seen on several nights and linked, and most early candidates will be asteroids or noise.
  3. 2028 to 2029
    The first real verdict. Two or three years of repeat imaging should reach most of the predicted brightness range1. Either a slow mover turns up, or only the faintest, farthest orbits and the stretch behind the Milky Way are left.
  4. meanwhile
    Side channels. A few deep images along the infrared candidate's predicted track would confirm or rule it out within a few months. A more sensitive repeat of the millimetre-wave search would look for the planet's own heat.
  5. —
    The survey ends. If nothing has been found, what remains is a planet fainter or farther than any current fit allows, or hidden in the Milky Way. The hypothesis as published would be close to ruled out.

What would confirm it

  • A faint dot seen on several nights, moving the way the predicted orbit says.
  • Found again the next year, which pins down its orbit.
  • Its mass comes last, from how it pulls on the orbits around it.

What would end it

  • Rubin covers the predicted path to the predicted depth and finds nothing.
  • The new distant orbits it finds point every which way.

Both together, not one failed search.

The telescope that could settle it

Finding a faint, slow-moving planet takes a telescope that sees faint enough and photographs enough of the sky, again and again. Until now, surveys have had one or the other.

How faint each survey can see

Each marker is the faintest magnitude one of a survey’s photos reaches; the percentage is the share of the whole sky it photographed, where published. Years are when it ran. The band is the range the models give for the planet1, not a measurement.

Some surveys covered much of the sky but could only detect brighter objects. Others could detect fainter objects but searched smaller areas. Finding Planet Nine requires both sufficient depth and coverage of the right locations.

What would settle the debate?

What would support Planet Nine?

  • A faint candidate found where the predictions allow.
  • Seen again on several nights, and the next season, moving the same slow way.
  • An orbit worked out from those sightings that fits the hypothesis.

What would weaken the case?

  • Searches deep enough to see the predicted planet cover its path and find nothing.
  • The new distant objects, found by the thousand, point every which way instead of lining up.

It would take both, not one empty search.
