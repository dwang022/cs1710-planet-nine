What comes next

The argument has gone on for a decade because the sky where the planet should be was never photographed deeply enough. That changed in 2026.

The telescope that could settle it

Every search so far stopped short of the planet's predicted brightness. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory's ten-year survey reaches past it over most of the sky where the planet could be, and photographs each patch again and again, which is what finding a slow-moving dot requires.

When could we know?

Dates after 2026 are expectations, not promises.

  1. June 2026
    Rubin's survey begins. The ten-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time started on 29 June 2026. By late October it had already turned up a probable new dwarf planet, 2026 ED138, the first of what should be tens of thousands of new distant objects.
  2. 2027
    Most of the southern path photographed deeply at least once. One deep image is not a detection: the planet would move only a few arcseconds a night, so it has to be seen on several nights and linked. Candidates will appear, and most will be ordinary asteroids or noise.
  3. 2028 to 2029
    The first real verdict. With two or three years of repeat imaging, Brown and Batygin's estimate is that Rubin reaches most of the predicted brightness range. Either a slow mover with the right motion turns up, or the predicted orbit is left with only its faintest, most distant corner and the stretch behind the Milky Way.
  4. meanwhile
    Side channels. A few deep pointings along the infrared candidate's predicted track (DECam or Subaru) would confirm or kill it within a season. The Simons Observatory could repeat the millimetre-wave search for the planet's own heat with far more sensitivity, independent of how much sunlight it reflects.
  5. 2036
    The survey ends. If nothing has been found by then, what remains is a planet fainter or farther than any current fit allows, or hidden against the dense star fields of the galactic plane. At that point the hypothesis as published would be close to ruled out.

What would confirm it

A faint object seen on several nights, moving a few arcseconds a day in the direction and at the rate the orbit predicts, then recovered the following season. A second sighting fixes the distance; a season of positions fixes the orbit; a mass comes last, from how it bends the orbits around it. Discovery would be announced through the Minor Planet Center like every other object on this site.

What would end it

Not a single failed search, but two things together: Rubin covering the predicted path to its predicted depth and finding nothing, and the tens of thousands of new distant objects it finds showing no preference for one direction. If the newly found extreme orbits scatter evenly, there is nothing left for a planet to explain.

The end, for now

Planet Nine is an inference, not an observation: a few dozen faint orbits that lean the same way, a planet that would explain it, and a decade of looking that has not found it but has not ruled it out. The next few years of Rubin data should do one or the other. Every number on this site will be refreshed as they arrive.
