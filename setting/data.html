The data

How these objects are found and measured, where the records are kept, and how few of the thousands of catalogued bodies actually matter.

How they are found

The method has not changed since Pluto: photograph the same patch of sky more than once and look for the one dot that moved. These are the actual frames in which four of the objects on this site were found.

From a few dots to an orbit

1
Find the mover. Two or three exposures, an hour or a week apart. Anything that shifts against the stars is in the solar system; how fast it shifts says roughly how far away it is.
2
Follow it. Each later night adds a position. The run of positions is the object's arc. Objects this far out barely move, so a useful arc takes years, and many are lost before they get one.
3
Fit the oval. An orbit is the one ellipse that passes through every measured position. With a short arc, many different ellipses fit almost equally well, so the far end of the orbit is guesswork.

A short arc is an uncertain orbit

Each dot is one of the 90 extreme objects. Across: how long it has been watched. Up: how much the two catalogues disagree about how far out its orbit goes. Both fit the same photographs; they differ only in method.

After a year or more of observations the two catalogues agree to within a few percent, often far better. Under a year, they can differ by a factor of two or twenty. That is why this site counts only orbits watched for at least a year, across three or more seasons, with a low formal uncertainty. — of the 90 fail that test.

Where the data lives

Every number on this site about a real object comes from two public catalogues that keep the orbits of everything known in the solar system. Here is the same object, Sedna, exactly as each of them stores it.

The two catalogues share the raw observations (every measured position is reported to the Minor Planet Center, which publishes it) but fit orbits independently, which is what makes the disagreement chart above possible. This site's processed files join the two by designation; — of — bodies cross-match. Query details and every source are in data/SOURCES.md.

Which objects matter

The catalogues hold — bodies whose average distance from the Sun is beyond Neptune's 30 AU. Each cell below is one of them. The Planet Nine argument rests on a tiny corner of this grid.

    Why these cuts: beyond about 150 AU an orbit is too large for Neptune to have shaped it recently, and a closest approach beyond 30 AU keeps it clear of Neptune altogether. Those are the objects whose orbits remember whatever else is out there. The well-measured ones are the only ones whose direction can be trusted. The six at the top-left are the orbits the 2016 proposal was built on.
