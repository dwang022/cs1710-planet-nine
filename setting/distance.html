How far away

The objects at the centre of the argument are hundreds of times farther from the Sun than Earth.

Start at Earth

Earth orbits 1 AU from the Sun: about 150 million km, or eight minutes at the speed of light. That distance, the astronomical unit, is the ruler for everything on this site.

Mercury, Venus and Mars all lie within two of them.

2 / 7

The giant planets

Jupiter orbits at about 5 AU and Saturn near 10. The four rocky planets are already crowding toward the Sun's glow.

3 / 7

The edge of the familiar

Uranus at 19 AU, Neptune at 30 AU. Sunlight takes four hours to reach Neptune. All eight planets fit inside this circle.

4 / 7

Pluto and the Kuiper belt

Pluto averages 39 AU, but its orbit is a stretched oval: it passes inside Neptune's orbit at one end and reaches 49 AU at the other. The region it shares with thousands of smaller icy bodies is the Kuiper belt.

The extreme objects

Each bar below the planets is one of the — well-measured distant objects this site is about, drawn from its nearest point to the Sun to its farthest, and stacked by how far out it goes. The dot is where the object is right now.

Nearly every dot sits at the near end. These objects were all caught close to the Sun, because that is the only place they are bright enough to see.

Point at a bar to see its numbers.

6 / 7

Where Planet Nine would be

The prediction is a range, not a point. On its best-fit orbit the planet would travel between about 320 and 600 AU from the Sun. Allowing for the uncertainty in that orbit, it could be anywhere from roughly 200 to 900 AU out. At those distances sunlight takes days to arrive, and very little of it is reflected back.

The farthest of them

The most distant of these objects reaches — from the Sun at the far end of its orbit. At this scale the eight planets sit inside the Sun's glow at the left edge.

AU: the ruler

One AU is Earth to the Sun, about 93 million miles. Neptune is 30 AU out. These objects reach hundreds. Voyager 1, after 48 years, is at about 170.

Orbits are ovals, not circles

Every object has a near end and a far end. It moves fast through the near end and crawls through the far one, so it spends most of its life far away, and dark.

Which way an orbit leans

Because the oval is long, it points somewhere. That direction, where the near end sits, is the single number the next part of this site is about.
