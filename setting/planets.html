Nine planets. Then eight. Now, perhaps, nine again

For 76 years the solar system had nine planets. In 2006 astronomers defined the word, and Pluto no longer qualified. Ten years later two of them argued that there is a ninth planet after all: not Pluto, but a world several times heavier than Earth, far beyond Neptune, that no one has seen.

The nine planets of the twentieth century

Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and Pluto.

Pluto, discovered in 1930, was by far the smallest. For sixty years it was the only known object in its part of the solar system, so its status was never questioned.

2 / 5

Pluto's neighbourhood fills up

From 1992 onward, surveys found many icy bodies in the same region as Pluto, now called the Kuiper belt. In 2005 one of them, Eris, turned out to be about Pluto's size, and was later shown to be more massive.

Either the list of planets would keep growing, or the word needed a definition.

3 / 5

2006: the IAU defines "planet"

A planet orbits the Sun, is massive enough to be round under its own gravity, and has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit.

Pluto meets the first two conditions. It fails the third: it shares its region with thousands of other bodies and is not even the most massive of them.

4 / 5

Eight planets, and a new category

On 24 August 2006 Pluto was reclassified as a dwarf planet, alongside Ceres and Eris. Nothing about Pluto changed; the definition did.

5 / 5

2016: a ninth planet is proposed

Two astronomers at Caltech noticed that several of the most distant known objects have orbits that appear to be aligned, as if a large unseen body were shaping them. They proposed Planet Nine: five to ten times Earth's mass, hundreds of times farther from the Sun than Neptune, and so far undetected.

If it exists, it satisfies all three conditions. Whether it exists is the question the rest of this site examines.

What counts as a planet

The International Astronomical Union's 2006 definition (Resolution B5, adopted 24 August 2006). A planet is a celestial body that:

  • 1
    Is in orbit around the Sun. Moons orbit a planet instead, so they are excluded at the first condition.
  • 2
    Is round under its own gravity. It has enough mass for its self-gravity to pull it into a nearly round shape, which the resolution calls hydrostatic equilibrium.
  • 3
    Has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit. It is the dominant body in its region, having swept up, ejected or captured most of what shared the orbit.A dwarf planet meets conditions 1 and 2 but not 3, and is not a moon. Everything else orbiting the Sun is a small body.
