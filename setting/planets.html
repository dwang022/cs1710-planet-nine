The nine planets of the twentieth century

  • Pluto, found in 1930, was by far the smallest.
  • For sixty years nothing else was known in its region, so its place was never questioned.
2 / 5

Pluto's neighbourhood fills up

  • From 1992, surveys found hundreds of icy bodies near Pluto: the Kuiper belt.
  • In 2005 one of them, Eris, turned out to be Pluto's size and heavier.

Either the list of planets keeps growing, or the word needs a definition.

3 / 5

2006: the IAU defines "planet"

A planet must:

  • orbit the Sun
  • be round under its own gravity
  • have cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit

Pluto fails the third: thousands of bodies share its region, one of them heavier.

4 / 5

Eight planets, and a new category

  • Pluto becomes a dwarf planet, alongside Ceres and Eris.
  • Nothing about Pluto changed. The definition did.
5 / 5

2016: a ninth planet is proposed

  • Two Caltech astronomers noticed that the most distant known orbits lean the same way, as if something large were steering them.
  • Their answer: a planet of five to ten Earth masses, hundreds of times farther out than Neptune. Never seen.

If it is there, it passes all three tests. Whether it is there is the rest of this site.

What counts as a planet

The International Astronomical Union's 2006 definition (Resolution B5, adopted 24 August 2006). A planet is a celestial body that:

  • 1
    Is in orbit around the Sun. Moons orbit a planet instead, so they are excluded at the first condition.
  • 2
    Is round under its own gravity. It has enough mass for its self-gravity to pull it into a nearly round shape, which the resolution calls hydrostatic equilibrium.
  • 3
    Has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit. It is the dominant body in its region, having swept up, ejected or captured most of what shared the orbit.A dwarf planet meets conditions 1 and 2 but not 3, and is not a moon. Everything else orbiting the Sun is a small body.
